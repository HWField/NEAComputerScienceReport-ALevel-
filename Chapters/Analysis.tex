\section{Introduction to the Problem}
Understanding how infectious diseases spread through urban environments is a complex and urgent problem, especially in light of recent global health crises. During pandemics, decision-makers rely on accurate models to evaluate the effectiveness of public health interventions such as social distancing, lockdowns, and vaccination however, many existing simulations operate at national or global scales, often lacking the granularity needed to inform decisions at the community level.

This project seeks to address this gap by simulating disease spread within a city-sized environment, consisting of approximately 100,000 agents (representing individuals) and 20,000 nodes (representing buildings or gathering points). The problem being tackled is how to model and visualize disease transmission within a realistic, localized setting, so that the impact of specific strategies (e.g. closing schools or limiting public transport) can be analysed.

The aim is to design and implement a simulation that accurately reflects how a disease could spread through a densely populated urban area, based on various parameters such as agent mobility, infection rate, and building density. The solution will not only allow for analysis of intervention strategies, but also provide a clear, visual representation of disease progression over time, making the data accessible and informative for both experts and the public.

This gives the project a secondary goal of being an educational tool: helping users understand the dynamics of disease spread and the potential impact of public health measures, however small and simple they may be. By combining agent-based modelling with real-world data, the project aims to create a flexible, user-friendly platform that can be used by researchers, policymakers, and the public to explore epidemic scenarios in a city context.

This project combines elements of agent-based modelling, data visualization, and algorithmic design to address a real-world problem through computational means.

\subsection*{Summary of Conversations}

Based on interviews with domain experts (Dr Faille and Dr Jin), general simulation user (Mr Field), and a general public user (Sally), the following key insights were identified to guide the design of the epidemic simulation tool:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Realism vs simplification:} Core interactions and transmission rules must be realistic to produce meaningful results, while environmental details (e.g., exact city layouts) can be simplified. Grouping similar data items or compartments helps reduce complexity without losing interpretability.
  \item \textbf{Parameter selection and sensitivity:} Parameters should be justified using prior knowledge, literature, or reasonable approximations. Sensitivity analysis is critical to understand how variations in parameters impact outcomes.
  \item \textbf{Validation and reproducibility:} Simulation outputs must be validated against known behaviours or real-world data. Repeated runs should produce consistent results, and emergent properties (e.g., bell curves of infections) serve as sanity checks.
  \item \textbf{Assumptions and limitations:} Clearly documenting assumptions, data sources, and simplifications is essential to avoid over-interpreting results. Public-facing tools should explain key parameters and normal operational ranges.
  \item \textbf{Usefulness of the tool:} A genuinely useful simulation allows scenario exploration, comparison with real-world data, regression testing, and the ability to quickly model changes. Technical sophistication alone does not ensure value.
  \item \textbf{Data visualisation:} Visual representation of results is critical. Graphs, charts, and clear visual cues support understanding of complex or large-scale outputs. Overlaying scenarios or showing trends over time enhances interpretability.
  \item \textbf{Educational and communication value:} Simplified models remain useful for learning and public communication, provided key assumptions are valid and clearly presented.
  \item \textbf{Usability for non-specialists:} Interfaces should be intuitive, interactive, and visually clear, enabling users with minimal technical background to adjust parameters, rerun simulations quickly, and explore “what if” scenarios.
  \item \textbf{Performance considerations:} Complex simulations may run slowly on local devices. Centralised or server-based computation can improve speed, allow multiple users to access the simulation, and support more computationally intensive models.
  \item \textbf{Public engagement and safeguards:} Tools for general use should include restricted parameter changes, explanations of model limitations, and guidance to prevent unrealistic assumptions, ensuring responsible and informative exploration.
\end{itemize}
I will use this research to inform my project requirements and will now explore some of the existing software in this field to identify gaps and opportunities for my project to fill.

GLEaMviz (Global Epidemic and Mobility Model) is a computational tool designed to simulate the spread of infectious diseases on a global scale by integrating real-world data on population, human mobility, and disease dynamics. It combines a metapopulation approach, dividing the world into subpopulations connected by transportation networks, with stochastic compartmental models (e.g., SEIR) to simulate disease progression and transmission. \subsubsection*{Algebraic frameworks for structured epidemic modelling}
\textbf{Source:} \autocite{libkindAlgebraicFrameworkStructured2022}

This paper gives an in-depth view to them mathematical modelling of epidemics. It provides a number of formulas and mathematical ideas that can use the gathered, compartmented data to create a scalable model of infection spread.

equations such as:


\[
    u_s = \sum_{t \in p(s)} \phi_t - \sum_{t \in r^{-1}(s)} \phi_t, \quad \text{where} \quad \phi_t := \beta_t \prod_{s \in r(t)} u_s
\]

\begin{center}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{\( u_s \)}: The value at node or state \( s \); represents something like the number or probability of individuals in a particular disease state (e.g., infected or susceptible).

        \item \textbf{\( p(s) \)}: The set of transitions \( t \) that lead \emph{into} state \( s \) (postconditions).

        \item \textbf{\( r^{-1}(s) \)}: The set of transitions \( t \) that lead \emph{out of} state \( s \) (preconditions).

        \item \textbf{\( \sum_{t \in p(s)} \phi_t \)}: The total inflow into state \( s \), contributed by transitions entering that state.

        \item \textbf{\( \sum_{t \in r^{-1}(s)} \phi_t \)}: The total outflow from state \( s \), due to transitions exiting that state.

        \item \textbf{\( \phi_t \)}: The value or "flow" associated with transition \( t \); represents how much effect the transition has.

        \item \textbf{\( \beta_t \)}: A rate constant for transition \( t \); models the likelihood or strength of the transition (e.g., infection rate).

        \item \textbf{\( r(t) \)}: The set of input states (preconditions) required for transition \( t \) to occur.

        \item \textbf{\( \prod_{s \in r(t)} u_s \)}: A product of the values of all input states for transition \( t \); shows how multiple states contribute multiplicatively to the transition (e.g., an infection requires both a susceptible and an infected individual).
    \end{itemize}
\end{center}
model how the value at a node (e.g. the number of people in a certain disease state) changes based on the rates of transitions going into and out of it. It's a generalized form of a flow equation, similar to what's seen in SIR-type epidemiological models, but more flexible and structured to fit into computational frameworks like agent-based models or Petri nets. Whilst equations like these are not directly applicable to my project, they do provide a strong mathematical basis for the algorithms I will need to implement to simulate disease spread. I will likely need to use some of these equations to calculate the rate of infection and recovery in my simulation.

\subsection{challenges of using data from multiple sources}
\textbf{Source:} \autocite{deangelisFourKeyChallenges2015}

This paper discusses both the benefits and negatives the use of multiple data sources can have. Benefits include a greater accuracy in the population spread such as covering a wider demographic. This can be a huge positive for a simulation as it may pickup on details previously missed by other data sets.

However it can also come with a number of challenges such as::
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Data Quality}: Different sources may have varying levels of accuracy, completeness, and reliability. Inconsistent data quality can lead to misleading results.
    \item \textbf{Data Integration}: Combining data from multiple sources often requires complex integration processes, which can introduce errors or biases if not done carefully.
    \item \textbf{Data Compatibility}: Different datasets may use different formats, units, or definitions, making it challenging to align them for analysis and risk double-counting entities skewing the results.
    \item \textbf{Data Timeliness}: Data from different sources may be collected at different times, leading to discrepancies in the temporal relevance of the information.
    \end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{Agent Parameters}
For each agent in the simulation I will need to generate a unique medical profile, daily routine and intention state system. These will each be fairly simplistic in nature to reduce compute time per simulation tick however the large quantity of agents at once will ensure the overall result is realistic. The parameteters I will need to model for each agent are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Age
    \item Immunity Level
    \item ChronicConditions
    \item InfectionSucceptibility
\end{itemize}
Additionally each agent will have a daily routine which will determine their movement around the map. This will be based on their age and role in society. To help seperate agents into these categories I will assign each a role from the list below:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Student
    \item Office worker
    \item Medical worker
    \item Retail worker
    \item Elderly / Retired
    \item Service worker
\end{itemize}

Whilst this list doesn't cover every member of society it enables me to simulate the vast majority of influential events that occur between population members.

The user will be able to either select a population preset (based on the map area they have chosen) or customise the percentage of each role in the population to simulate different areas (e.g. a university town will have a higher percentage of students than a rural village). Each role will have a predefined daily routine which can be tweaked by the user if desired. For example, a student may have a routine that includes going to school, studying, and socialising with friends, whereas an elderly person may have a routine that includes going to the park, visiting family, and attending community events.

Each routine will be generated at system setup however agents have the flexability to deviate from this routine based on their intention state. For example, if an agent is feeling unwell they may choose to stay home instead of going to work or school. This will be determined by a simple intention state system which will be influenced by the agent's health status and the current state of the epidemic.

\subsection{Simulation Server}
With such a large number of agents being simulated I was aware that this would have a heavy compute premium, especially since I wanted the simulation to simulate at a rate of 1 virtual day per couple of real world seconds. Unsure whether my preferred programming language of python would be suitable I created a simple benchmark which loaded 100,000 agents and 50,000 nodes into memory and simulated them each being assigned a new location and potentially a new state (infected, cured etc) per tick.
In my first python iteration, which used large lists for all data, the data calculated at 1.5 Ticks per second which was far too slow to be useful, bearing in mind this benchmark was very simplistic and purely focused on randomising movement for each agent this was glacial.
To try and remain within the python ecosystem I looked to implement python numba to handle such large variables. I found that this tool hugely improved the performance (to 211 ticks / s) but hugely increased system memory usage (4x non numba) which would make my backend system non-scalable, the codebase also became less readable / maintainable

Concerned with low performance (even with the addition of numba) I decided to recreate my program in C\# - a compiled language. I found an immediate, huge improvement to 1599 Ticks per second with code that was functionally the same differing only in language. These huge performance improvements therefore justified switching my backend stack to focus around the .NET ecosystem rather than python. I will use ASP.NET core to create my API and a WebSocket server to stream real time updates to the client. The backend simulation engine will be written in C\# to maximise performance as mentioned prior and the use of EFCore will help me create a performant and stable database.

\subsection{Data Storage and streaming}
With C\# simulation layer and API endpoints planned I needed to test a method to stream data between the two for real time previewing and longer term storage.

Initially I thought to store every simulation movement and path trace etc inside a Postgres DB however this resulted in my device resource utilisation spiking and C\# ticks per second falling to 12 due to the heavy read/write penalty of interacting with so many queries whilst also mutating so much data, even with batch processing I only gained a couple more Ticks per second. 

Testing with python then reading the data in real-time caused the Ticks/s to fall so low my 10minute run only parsed 3 ticks - completely unusable. It was at this point I came to the conclusion that whilst real-time streaming of the entire simulation would not be possible, that wasn't necessarily an issue. Such a large amount of data to populate to the user would definitely have hit web socket limitations and previewing 100,000 agents in a useful way would have been next to impossible. I therefore decided to only stream key statistics to the user such as number of infected, recovered, susceptible etc in addition to streaming a small subset of agents (e.g. 1000) to show movement and spread. This would be far more useful to the user and would allow me to store all data in a memory cache (which batch writes to a database) for post simulation analysis without impacting the simulation performance.

Should a user want more detailed data they can always download the entire simulation data set for offline analysis.

\subsection{Tick Sequence}
The fundemantal time span of my simulation is a 'tick' which represents a period of time in which the simulation runs and agents can move, interact and change state. The tick sequence is as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Agent Movement:} Each agent moves to a new location based on their routine and the current state of the simulation. This movement is influenced by their role (e.g., student, worker) and the locations of buildings in the simulation.
    \item \textbf{Disease Spread Calculation:} After all agents have moved, the simulation calculates the spread of disease based on the current state of each agent and the disease profile. This includes determining which agents become infected, recover, or remain susceptible.
    \item \textbf{State Update:} Each agent's state is updated based on the disease spread calculation. This may involve changing their health status (e.g., from susceptible to infected) and updating their health statistics (e.g., R0, recovery rate).
    \item \textbf{Data Storage:} The current state of the simulation, including agent locations and health statistics, is stored in the database for later analysis. This allows users to review the simulation results and visualize the spread of disease over time.
    \item \textbf{User Interaction:} Users can interact with the simulation by pausing, resuming, or scrubbing through the timeline. They can also apply interventions, such as closing buildings or enforcing policies, which will affect the next tick's agent movement and disease spread.
\end{enumerate}

Choosing the duration of a tick is a difficult balancing act as i want to maximise simulation precision whilst also maintaining acceptable simulation speed. For example a tick time of 1minute would allow for a huge number of agent interactions and an excellent 'slow motion' effect for the viewer however would take much longer to compute, inversely a tick duration of an entire day would result in missing the nuance of E.g. going to collect a meal and interacting with other agents in the same venue. I have therefore settled on a tick duration of 30 minutes which allows for a good balance of agent interaction and simulation speed. This duration allows for enough time for agents to move around, interact with others, and for the disease to spread realistically, while still being fast enough to keep the simulation engaging and responsive to user input. Should a user wish to view the simulation at a high speed each day will only involve 48 ticks (a week would be 336 ticks) which my testing found to be a quick computation time enabling multi month and year long simulation to be run in minutes rather than hours.
